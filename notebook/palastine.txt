The Palestinian People

The Palestinian people are an Arab people with deep historical, cultural, and social connections to the land of Palestine. Palestinian communities exist in the West Bank, the Gaza Strip, East Jerusalem, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and many other countries around the world. Palestinians have developed diverse communities while maintaining connections through family, language, culture, history, and traditions.

Palestinian history is connected to the wider history of the eastern Mediterranean and the Middle East. Over many centuries, the region was home to different communities, religions, languages, and political authorities. Palestinian society developed through villages, towns, and cities, with agriculture, trade, education, religious institutions, and local communities playing important roles.

Palestinian culture includes a rich tradition of food, music, embroidery, poetry, storytelling, architecture, and dance. One well-known traditional dance is the dabke, which is commonly performed at weddings, celebrations, and cultural events. Palestinian embroidery, known as tatreez, is another important cultural tradition. Different regions have developed distinctive embroidery patterns, colors, and designs, and these patterns have been passed from one generation to another.

Food is also an important part of Palestinian cultural identity. Traditional dishes include maqluba, musakhan, mansaf in some communities, falafel, hummus, tabbouleh, and various breads and pastries. Recipes often vary between families and regions. Food is frequently associated with hospitality, family gatherings, religious occasions, and celebrations.

The Arabic language is central to Palestinian cultural life. Palestinian Arabic has regional variations and is part of the broader family of Arabic dialects spoken throughout the Levant. Palestinian writers, poets, journalists, academics, and artists have contributed to Arabic literature and cultural expression. Poetry has played an especially important role in expressing personal experiences, historical memory, identity, displacement, and hopes for the future.

The modern political history of the Palestinians has been shaped by major events during the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. The end of the Ottoman period, the British Mandate, the establishment of Israel in 1948, the displacement of many Palestinians during the 1948 war, the 1967 war, and subsequent political developments have had major effects on Palestinian society.

The events of 1948 are remembered by Palestinians as the Nakba, meaning catastrophe. During and around the 1948 war, hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were displaced from their homes. Many became refugees in neighboring countries, while others remained within the area that became Israel. The descendants of these refugees continue to live in refugee communities and cities across the region and elsewhere.

The West Bank and Gaza Strip have also experienced significant political and social changes. Following the 1967 war, Israel occupied the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip. The political and legal status of these territories has remained one of the central issues of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

The Palestinian national movement has included different political organizations and perspectives. The Palestine Liberation Organization, or PLO, became an important representative organization of Palestinians during the twentieth century. In 1993, Israel and the PLO signed the Oslo Accords, which created the Palestinian Authority and established a framework for limited Palestinian self-government in parts of the West Bank and Gaza.

The Palestinian Authority administers certain areas of the West Bank under the arrangements created through the Oslo process. Hamas has controlled the Gaza Strip since 2007. Palestinian politics therefore include different political movements, institutions, and viewpoints, and Palestinians do not all share the same political opinions.

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict has involved wars, military occupation, displacement, attacks against civilians, settlement activity, political negotiations, protests, and international diplomatic efforts. The conflict has caused significant human suffering among both Palestinians and Israelis. Questions concerning borders, Jerusalem, settlements, refugees, security, sovereignty, and the political status of the Palestinian territories remain central to efforts to resolve the conflict.

Palestinian refugees form a large part of the Palestinian population. Many families have preserved memories of towns and villages from which their ancestors were displaced. Refugee communities can be found in the West Bank, Gaza, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria. The issue of Palestinian refugees and the question of a possible right of return are among the major subjects in negotiations over a potential political settlement.

Jerusalem has particular importance for Palestinians, Israelis, Muslims, Christians, and Jews. East Jerusalem is claimed by Palestinians as the capital of a future Palestinian state, while Israel considers Jerusalem its capital. The city's religious and political significance makes it one of the most sensitive subjects in the conflict.

Education has historically been important in Palestinian society. Palestinian students attend schools and universities in the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, Israel, and other countries. Palestinian universities and educational institutions have contributed to research, professional education, medicine, engineering, arts, and the humanities.

Palestinian society is diverse. Palestinians include Muslims and Christians as well as people with different political, social, and cultural backgrounds. Palestinian Christians have longstanding communities in cities and towns including Bethlehem, Jerusalem, Ramallah, and other areas. Religious traditions, family relationships, local customs, and regional identities contribute to the diversity of Palestinian society.

The Palestinian diaspora is another important part of Palestinian identity. Large Palestinian communities live in Jordan, Lebanon, Chile, the United States, Canada, Europe, and countries in the Gulf region. Palestinians living outside historic Palestine often maintain connections with relatives, cultural traditions, food, language, and family histories.

Art has provided another way for Palestinians to express identity and experience. Palestinian painters, photographers, filmmakers, musicians, and contemporary artists have explored themes such as home, memory, displacement, family, landscape, and everyday life. Cultural institutions and individual artists have worked to preserve Palestinian heritage and introduce Palestinian stories to international audiences.

Literature is also significant. Palestinian authors and poets have written about displacement, homeland, exile, political conflict, family life, and personal experiences. Their work appears in Arabic and in many translated languages. Mahmoud Darwish, for example, became one of the most internationally recognized Palestinian poets. His writing explored identity, exile, memory, love, and homeland.

Palestinian civil society includes humanitarian organizations, charities, professional associations, educational institutions, cultural groups, and community organizations. These organizations work on issues including education, healthcare, human rights, economic development, cultural preservation, and assistance to vulnerable communities.

The humanitarian situation in Palestinian territories has changed over time and has been particularly severe during periods of armed conflict. In Gaza, restrictions, economic difficulties, infrastructure damage, and repeated conflicts have affected everyday life. In the West Bank, Palestinians experience different conditions depending on where they live, including issues related to movement, access to land, security, settlements, and administrative control.

The October 2023 attack by Hamas and other armed groups in southern Israel, which killed civilians and resulted in the taking of hostages, was followed by a major Israeli military campaign in Gaza. The subsequent war caused extensive civilian casualties, displacement, destruction of homes and infrastructure, and a severe humanitarian crisis in Gaza. Israeli authorities have stated that military operations are aimed at Hamas and other armed groups and that security and the return of hostages are central objectives. International organizations have documented extensive humanitarian needs among Palestinians in Gaza.

Different governments, international organizations, and political groups have proposed different approaches to resolving the conflict. One widely discussed framework is a two-state solution involving Israel and a Palestinian state. Other proposals and political perspectives differ regarding borders, sovereignty, security arrangements, Jerusalem, settlements, refugees, and the relationship between the two peoples.

Understanding Palestinian history and society requires recognizing that Palestinians are not a single political group. Individuals have different political opinions, religious beliefs, experiences, and views about the future. Some support negotiations and a two-state solution, while others support different political arrangements. Palestinian communities also differ according to geography, generation, family history, and experiences of displacement.

For many Palestinians, identity is connected to family, language, culture, memories of places, and relationships with communities. For people living in refugee camps or in the diaspora, stories about villages and cities connected to previous generations can remain an important part of family history. At the same time, younger Palestinians have contemporary experiences shaped by education, technology, employment, migration, and global culture.

The Palestinian people have therefore developed a complex modern identity shaped by history, culture, displacement, political conflict, and everyday life. Their cultural traditions continue through families, schools, communities, literature, music, food, art, and social organizations. Palestinians around the world continue to debate political questions while maintaining connections to their heritage and communities.

Studying Palestinian history benefits from examining multiple sources and perspectives. Historical records, academic research, international organizations, Israeli and Palestinian sources, journalism, oral histories, and personal accounts can provide different information and interpretations. Because many aspects of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are politically contested, it is important to distinguish documented historical events from interpretations, political claims, and individual experiences.

The history of the Palestinian people continues to develop. Political negotiations, regional events, international diplomacy, humanitarian conditions, and developments within Palestinian and Israeli societies continue to influence the future. Regardless of political perspective, Palestinian history and culture remain important subjects for understanding the modern Middle East.
